# Weather vs Climate: Fundamental Concepts

## Definitions and Temporal Scales
The fundamental distinction between weather and climate lies in the dimension of time and statistical aggregation:
- **Weather** describes the specific state of the atmosphere at a particular time and place. It involves short-term variations occurring over minutes, hours, days, or weeks. Meteorological variables defining weather include instantaneous temperature, atmospheric pressure, relative humidity, cloud cover, wind speed and direction, visibility, and precipitation.
- **Climate** represents the statistical description of weather conditions over an extended temporal baseline—conventionally established by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) as a 30-year period (known as "climatological normals"). Climate encapsulates not only average conditions (mean values) but also the frequency, intensity, and probability distributions of extreme weather events, variability, and recurring cyclical anomalies (such as El Niño-Southern Oscillation).

## Dynamics and Predictability Horizons
The atmospheric dynamics governing weather are inherently chaotic, described by non-linear Navier-Stokes fluid equations with sensitive dependence on initial conditions (often termed the "butterfly effect"). Consequently, deterministic weather forecasting is fundamentally constrained by hydrodynamic limits to approximately 10 to 14 days, beyond which individual forecast errors grow to match the climatological variance.

In contrast, climate projections operate on boundary-forced systems rather than initial-value sensitivity. Climate is determined by thermodynamic balances between incoming solar radiation, planetary albedo, greenhouse gas concentrations, ocean-atmosphere heat exchange, and volcanic or solar forcing. While meteorologists cannot predict the specific rainfall on a particular afternoon 30 years from now, climatologists can assess changes in the broader probability distribution of precipitation and average seasonal temperatures driven by systematic radiative forcing.

## Common Misconceptions
A single unseasonably cold winter day or an isolated record heatwave does not prove or disprove broader climatic shifts. Weather events represent individual draws from a statistical probability distribution; climate defines the shape, position, and extremes of that distribution.
