# Tropical Cyclones in the North Indian Ocean

## Basin Dynamics and Seasonality
Tropical cyclones in the North Indian Ocean (comprising the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea) exhibit a unique bimodal seasonal distribution, peaking during the pre-monsoon transition (April to May) and post-monsoon transition (October to December). During the peak southwest monsoon (June to September), high vertical wind shear associated with the Tropical Easterly Jet prevents warm convective systems from organizing into deep cyclonic vortices, despite very warm sea surface temperatures.

Historically, the Bay of Bengal experiences approximately 4 to 5 times more cyclones than the Arabian Sea. The Bay of Bengal is characterized by shallow bathymetry, higher sea surface temperatures (frequently exceeding 28°C–29°C), high upper-ocean thermal energy, and substantial freshwater discharge from major river systems (Ganges, Brahmaputra, Meghna, Godavari), which creates strong salinity stratification and inhibits cold-water upwelling. However, warming trends in recent decades have increased the frequency of severe cyclonic storms in the Arabian Sea.

## Genesis Criteria and Classification
Tropical cyclogenesis requires several thermodynamic and dynamic preconditions:
1. Sea surface temperatures (SST) exceeding 26.5°C to a depth of at least 50 meters.
2. Low vertical wind shear (less than 10–15 knots) between the lower troposphere (850 hPa) and upper troposphere (200 hPa).
3. Sufficient Coriolis acceleration, typically requiring genesis locations at least 5 degrees of latitude away from the equator.
4. Pre-existing low-level cyclonic vorticity and high mid-tropospheric (500–700 hPa) relative humidity.

The India Meteorological Department (IMD), which serves as the WMO Regional Specialized Meteorological Centre (RSMC) for Tropical Cyclones over the North Indian Ocean, categorizes cyclonic disturbances based on 3-minute sustained surface wind speeds:
- Depression: 31–49 km/h (17–27 knots)
- Deep Depression: 50–61 km/h (28–33 knots)
- Cyclonic Storm: 62–87 km/h (34–47 knots)
- Severe Cyclonic Storm: 88–117 km/h (48–63 knots)
- Very Severe Cyclonic Storm: 118–165 km/h (64–89 knots)
- Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm: 166–220 km/h (90–119 knots)
- Super Cyclonic Storm: Winds 221 km/h or higher (120 knots and above)

## Primary Hazards and Impacts
The hazards posed by tropical cyclones include storm surges, destructive inland wind forces, and extreme precipitation. Storm surge—the astronomical tide coupled with ocean water driven ashore by low atmospheric pressure and intense onshore winds—is responsible for the majority of coastal casualties, particularly along the concave coastline and low-elevation delta plains of eastern India and Bangladesh.
