# The Northeast (Post-Monsoon) Season

## Withdrawal Dynamics
Beginning in September, declining solar declination cools the northern Eurasian and Tibetan landmass, causing atmospheric pressure to rise rapidly across northwest India and Central Asia. The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) retreats southward toward the equatorial Indian Ocean. This shift causes the low-level summer westerlies to dissipate and transition into dry, cool northeasterly continental trade winds originating from the Siberian and Tibetan high-pressure ridges.

By mid-October, the southwest monsoon completely withdraws from northern and central India, ushering in the post-monsoon transition (historically associated with pleasant "October heat" as clear skies increase insolation while residual soil moisture remains elevated).

## Rainfall Distribution and Peninsular Significance
While the Northeast Monsoon brings dry, cool, cloud-free weather to north, central, and western India (including Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the Gangetic plains), it plays a vital agricultural role for southeastern peninsular India:
- As the northeasterly winds blow across the warm, open waters of the Bay of Bengal, they absorb substantial latent heat and moisture.
- These moisture-laden easterly winds make landfall on the Coromandel Coast, striking the Eastern Ghats and coastal plains.
- Between October and December, this circulation provides 50% to 60% of the total annual precipitation for the state of **Tamil Nadu**, coastal Andhra Pradesh, Rayalaseema, and adjoining portions of Kerala and interior southern Karnataka.

## Tropical Cyclogenesis Peak
The post-monsoon period (October–November) represents the primary annual peak in tropical cyclonic activity in the North Indian Ocean, especially across the Bay of Bengal, due to warm sea surface temperatures ($\ge 28^\circ\text{C}$), minimal vertical wind shear, and active convergence along the southward-migrating ITCZ.
